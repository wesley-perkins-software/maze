Not all mazes are square grids. While the standard rectangular grid maze is the most common — partly because it's the easiest to print and navigate — there's a rich variety of maze structures based on different tessellations and shapes. Each type creates a distinct solving experience.

## Orthogonal (Rectangular Grid) Mazes

The standard maze. Cells are squares arranged in a grid, with four possible connections per cell: north, south, east, and west. This is the format used by the vast majority of printed maze puzzles and the type MazeThis generates.

**Navigation:** Intuitive — movement maps directly to up/down/left/right. Solvers can easily track their position relative to the grid's edges.

**Strengths:** Easy to read, easy to print, works well at any scale. The familiar grid structure means solvers can use systematic strategies efficiently.

**Limitations:** Can start to feel formulaic with experience. Experienced maze-solvers find that orthogonal mazes at a given size feel somewhat predictable compared to non-rectangular alternatives.

**Best for:** All ages and skill levels. The format of choice for printed worksheet mazes.

## Hexagonal (Sigma) Mazes

Instead of squares, hexagonal mazes use regular hexagons as cells. Each cell has six potential neighbors (compared to four in an orthogonal maze), connected via six possible wall openings.

The navigation feel is notably different: instead of pure cardinal directions, movement is diagonal-feeling — northwest, northeast, east, southeast, southwest, west. This makes it harder to track orientation and easy to feel genuinely disoriented.

**Visual appearance:** Hexagonal mazes have a distinctive honeycomb aesthetic that looks more organic than square grids. They're immediately recognizable and feel more unusual.

**Navigation difficulty:** Significantly harder than equivalent orthogonal mazes, because the extra connectivity options mean more branching and more decision points per unit of path length. The hexagonal grid also disrupts simple strategies like the right-hand rule.

**Best for:** Experienced maze-solvers looking for a fresh challenge, or any context where visual distinctiveness matters.

## Circular (Theta) Mazes

Circular mazes arrange cells in concentric rings, with each ring divided into arc-shaped cells. Connections exist both between adjacent cells in the same ring (around the circle) and between adjacent rings (toward/away from the center).

The center is typically the exit (or entry), giving these mazes a distinctive challenge: find your way to the middle.

**Navigation feel:** Disorienting in an interesting way. The circular structure disrupts the left-right/up-down mental model that solvers apply in orthogonal mazes. The innermost rings have fewer cells (sometimes just a few), creating a natural funnel.

**Visual appearance:** Beautiful on paper. Circular mazes look elegant and are immediately recognizable as unusual. They photograph and print well and have a high "wow factor."

**Solving strategy:** Many solvers find it helpful to divide a circular maze into quadrants and plan a route through each quadrant. The right-hand rule can work, but the circular structure requires careful tracking of direction.

**Best for:** Decorative contexts (posters, puzzle books), experienced solvers, visual novelty.

## Triangular (Delta) Mazes

Triangular mazes use equilateral triangle cells. Each cell has three sides, but the number of neighbors per cell alternates between two and three depending on the triangle's orientation (pointing up vs. pointing down).

**Navigation feel:** Highly disorienting. The triangular tessellation creates a maze that doesn't align with intuitive north/south/east/west thinking. Movement feels oblique and non-obvious.

**Visual appearance:** Unusual and visually interesting. Triangular grids look complex and slightly alien to most maze-solvers.

**Difficulty:** Typically harder than equivalent orthogonal mazes, because the non-intuitive geometry disrupts standard solving strategies.

**Best for:** Advanced solvers, novelty, or contexts where maximum visual distinctiveness is desired.

## Irregular (Shaped) Mazes

The maze doesn't have to fill a rectangle. Shaped mazes use custom outlines — the maze fills the shape of a star, an animal silhouette, a letter, a holiday icon, or any other form. The cells remain orthogonal (or hexagonal), but the outer boundary is irregular.

**Appeal:** Immediately visually engaging. A maze shaped like a pumpkin or a Christmas tree has novelty appeal that a plain rectangle doesn't. Kids in particular respond enthusiastically to shaped mazes.

**Difficulty:** Can vary widely. Shaped mazes sometimes have large dead-end regions near the extremities of the shape (the "tips" of a star, for instance) that make them harder than they appear at first. The irregular boundary disrupts wall-following strategies.

**Best for:** Holidays, themed activities, children's books, gifts with personalization.

## 3D and Multi-Level Mazes

These mazes include vertical connections — passages that lead up or down between levels. In print form, this requires a key or legend to explain which arrows or symbols represent up/down transitions.

**Navigation challenge:** Extremely disorienting. Holding a 3D mental map while navigating a 2D printed representation is cognitively demanding in a qualitatively different way from 2D navigation.

**Best for:** Advanced solvers, puzzle books targeting adults, or digital formats where the 3D nature can be visually represented.

## Weave Mazes

Weave mazes are orthogonal mazes with an additional feature: some passages can pass *over* or *under* other passages, like a bridge. This creates a topologically more complex structure where the maze is no longer planar.

**Visual appearance:** Weave mazes have a distinctive look where some corridors clearly cross over others. They're immediately recognizable as more complex than standard mazes.

**Navigation difficulty:** Very high. The over/under topology breaks all standard wall-following strategies and requires more careful mental tracking.

**Best for:** Expert-level printed mazes, puzzle books targeting dedicated maze enthusiasts.

## Choosing a Maze Type

For most purposes — especially printing for kids, classrooms, or general recreational use — **orthogonal mazes are the right choice**. They're universally understood, easy to navigate, and work at any skill level.

For experienced solvers who want a fresh challenge, hexagonal mazes offer significantly more disorientation at the same grid size. Circular mazes add visual appeal and a unique center-focused challenge.

The maze type is ultimately a design choice that trades off between accessibility, visual appeal, and navigation difficulty. A great maze site should eventually offer all of these — they serve different audiences and different purposes.
