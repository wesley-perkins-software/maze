Two mazes can have the same grid dimensions and look visually similar but feel dramatically different to solve. A 15×15 maze might take one person 30 seconds and another 5 minutes. Why? What actually makes a maze hard?

Maze difficulty is a function of several interacting variables — some obvious, some surprising. Understanding them helps you choose the right maze for your skill level and helps maze designers (and algorithms) create appropriately calibrated challenges.

## 1. Grid Size (Cells)

The most obvious factor. A 20×20 maze has 400 cells; a 10×10 has 100. More cells means more potential paths to explore and more territory to hold in working memory.

But size alone is misleading. A 20×20 maze with many extra openings (loops) might be easier to navigate than a 10×10 with no extra openings, because the loops create alternative routes that help solvers find their way out when stuck.

**The formula:** Difficulty scales roughly with the square of linear dimension — doubling the width and height creates a maze approximately four times harder (not two times), because both path length and dead-end count increase together.

## 2. Dead Ends (Branching Without Reward)

Dead ends are the primary mechanism of difficulty. A dead end is a path that looks promising but leads nowhere — it forces the solver to backtrack and try something else.

The number and depth of dead ends is the most important single variable in maze difficulty:

- **Kids mazes:** Very few dead ends; most paths lead somewhere useful
- **Easy mazes:** Moderate dead ends; typically short (1–3 cells before hitting a wall)
- **Medium mazes:** More frequent dead ends; some moderately deep (4–8 cells)
- **Hard mazes:** Maximum dead ends; many deep (10+ cells), elaborate false paths

A deep dead end is especially cruel: it looks exactly like a promising corridor for the first dozen cells, pulling the solver further and further before revealing that it goes nowhere. The solver then has to retrace their entire path back to the last junction.

**Technical measure:** Maze researchers quantify this as the "dead-end ratio" — the proportion of cells that are dead ends versus cells on the solution path. In a hard maze, this can be 80% or higher.

## 3. Solution Path Length (Relative to Grid Size)

If a maze is 20×20 (400 cells), the solution path could theoretically be anywhere from about 40 cells (a near-straight path) to 350+ cells (a winding path that visits nearly every cell).

A longer solution path means the solver has to navigate more junctions, make more correct decisions, and track a more complex route. Longer solutions are generally harder.

But there's a subtlety: a very long solution path in a small grid means the maze is *dense* — the correct path winds back and forth across the same area repeatedly. This is particularly disorienting because different parts of the solution look similar and the solver's mental model gets confused.

## 4. Branching Factor

Branching factor is the average number of choices a solver has at each junction. A maze with frequent three-way and four-way junctions has a higher branching factor than one where most junctions are simple two-way choices.

High branching factor means more opportunities to make the wrong choice, which means more backtracking. A maze with an average branching factor of 3 at each junction is substantially harder than one with an average of 2.

**The interplay:** High branching factor combined with deep dead ends is the recipe for maximum difficulty. Each junction looks like a real choice, and each wrong choice leads to a long, costly dead end.

## 5. Visual Complexity and Wall Density

Some mazes look more complex than they are; others look simpler. Visual complexity affects difficulty independent of the underlying graph structure.

A maze with very dense wall patterns — lots of short corridors close together — is harder to visually parse than one with more open areas. The solver's eye can't easily distinguish the solution corridor from adjacent dead-end corridors.

Conversely, mazes with extra wall removals (loops) look more complex but are actually easier to solve, because the loops create alternative routes when the solver gets stuck.

**Counterintuitive finding:** Mazes generated by the Recursive Backtracker algorithm (the type on MazeThis) look visually dense and complex, which makes them *feel* harder than they are. Prim's algorithm mazes often look simpler (more open) but can have similar or greater actual difficulty due to higher branching factor.

## 6. Entry and Exit Placement

Where the entry and exit are located significantly affects difficulty:

- **Diagonal placement** (entry top-left, exit bottom-right) creates maximum traversal requirement — the solver must navigate most of the maze
- **Same-wall placement** (both on the left wall, say) is generally easier because the overall direction of travel is clearer
- **Adjacent placement** (entry and exit very close together) can create a misleadingly simple-looking maze that requires a very long path to connect them

Standard maze convention places the entry at the top-left and exit at the bottom-right, which provides a consistent moderate difficulty baseline.

## How MazeThis Tunes Difficulty

MazeThis uses the Recursive Backtracker algorithm (which produces maximum dead-end density by default), then applies **post-processing wall removal** to achieve different difficulty levels:

| Difficulty | Extra Walls Removed | Effect |
|------------|--------------------|---------| 
| Kids | 30% | Very open; short dead ends; many alternative routes |
| Easy | 25% | Open paths; dead ends are short and obvious |
| Medium | 10% | Moderate dead-end frequency; some longer false paths |
| Adults | 5% | Dense structure; frequent backtracking required |
| Hard | 0% | Maximum dead ends; pure DFS maze; longest solution paths |

The grid sizes are also tuned by difficulty:

| Difficulty | Sizes Used |
|------------|-----------|
| Kids | 5×5, 6×6, 8×8 |
| Easy | 5×5, 8×8, 10×10 |
| Medium | 10×10, 12×12, 15×15 |
| Adults | 15×15, 20×20, 25×25 |
| Hard | 15×15, 20×20, 25×25 |

Adults and Hard use the same grid sizes but Hard has significantly more dead ends — two mazes of the same physical size can differ dramatically in feel.

## Finding Your Difficulty Sweet Spot

The right difficulty is one that requires genuine effort but yields satisfaction within your patience window. Some guidelines:

- A maze you solve in under 2 minutes is probably too easy (unless you're building speed)
- A maze you can't solve in 15 minutes without the hint is probably too hard
- A maze you solve in 5–10 minutes with some backtracking is in the sweet spot

Most people should start one level below where they think they belong. A 15×15 hard maze is significantly harder than it looks when you first see it.
