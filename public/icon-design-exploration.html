The violet problem
Why it isn't sitting right

Your instinct is correct. Violet and blue belong to the same color family — both are cool-toned, both sit in the blue-purple range. On a dense maze with black walls on white, your brain perceptually groups them together. The player trail and the hint path end up reading as two versions of the same thing rather than two categorically different things.

There's also a rendering issue: violet at typical stroke widths tends to look slightly muddy against dark walls, especially at small maze sizes. Blue's higher chroma reads more crisply. Purple hints look uncertain; they don't feel authoritative enough to say "follow me."

The deeper problem is temperature. Both blue and violet are cool colors. What you actually need for the hint path is something warm — a color from the opposite side of the wheel that can never be confused with the blue trail even in peripheral vision.

Diagnosis

The issue is color temperature, not hue. Violet ↔ Blue = same family, same feel. The hint path needs to be warm where the trail is cool — so they feel categorically different at a glance. This is not a subtle distinction; warm vs. cool is one of the most powerful separators in visual design.
The bigger question
Should gameplay colors match the homepage?

The homepage has a clear, strong visual identity: warm cream, charcoal ink, and a bold red-orange accent (#E03B24). It's editorial, architectural, intentional. The gameplay palette (blue trail, teal start, amber finish, violet hints) is completely disconnected — it looks like a generic puzzle game dropped inside a beautiful site.

They don't need to match exactly — gameplay and marketing surfaces serve different purposes. But they should feel like they belong to the same product. Right now they don't.

#E03B24 is used for the "Today's" hero headline, all CTA buttons, left-border status indicators, and accent dots. It's the one color that says MazeThis. Every other color on the homepage is neutral.

The most natural way to stitch gameplay into the brand: use the red-orange accent for hint and solution paths. On the homepage, that color means "here's the action you should take." In the game, a hint means the same thing — the game reaching in and saying "go this way." Same color, same meaning, different context.

The player trail staying blue is fine — --color-brand: #2563eb is your legacy gameplay color and it reads well. But the hint is the place where the site's actual brand voice can enter the game.

Answer

Yes, one gameplay color should connect to the homepage. The hint/solution path is the right candidate — it's the game's "voice," just like the CTA is the site's "voice." Using the same red-orange creates coherence without making the maze look like a marketing page.

Final recommendation

Switch hints to the brand red-orange

It solves the violet problem (warm vs. cool), it avoids the teal/green proximity issue from Option B, it avoids the orange/amber collision in Option C, and it makes the gameplay feel like it belongs to MazeThis rather than a generic puzzle app. One color doing four jobs at once.
